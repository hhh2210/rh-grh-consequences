## Cramér's short interval

Take the difference between two adjacent averages of $ψ$, each of width $h$, before taking absolute values in their explicit formulas. A zero $ρ$ on the critical line contributes at most $h/√x$ to this difference, uniformly in its height. The derivative of $t^ρ/ρ$ has norm $t^{-1/2}$. Multiplying by the number of zeros through height $T=x/h$, counted with multiplicity, bounds the low zeros by $O(√x \log x)$.

Above $T$, the inverse-square zero tail is $O((\log T)/T)$. Its coefficient in the two averages is $O(x^{3/2}/h)$, giving the same error. For $h=K√x \log x$ with $K$ sufficiently large, the increase in $ψ$ exceeds the possible prime-power contribution at the right endpoint. Thus $θ$ increases strictly, giving a prime in $(x,x+2h]$. [Lean proof](Cramer.lean) · [Dudek’s short-interval argument](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.6417)

## Dirichlet characters and small nonresidues

The two GRH predicates in `Ankeny.lean` use the archive's open-strip signatures on Mathlib's `DirichletCharacter.LFunction`. They are equivalent. Passing to an imprimitive character adds finite Euler factors that do not vanish in positive real part. The results here use GRH as a hypothesis. [Archive statements](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis/blob/40e56bab/src/Statements.lean) · [Ankeny reduction](Ankeny.lean)

At the Mathlib pin, the Dirichlet L-series has an Euler product and agrees with the analytic L-function for real part greater than one. The completed primitive L-function satisfies its functional equation with the inverse character, root number and conductor factor. The twisted von Mangoldt series is the negative logarithmic derivative in the half-plane of absolute convergence, and Mathlib proves nonvanishing through real part one for nonprincipal characters. For a nonprincipal character, the triangular sum weights each $n\le x$ by $\chi(n)\Lambda(n)(x-n)$. Its Perron kernel is $K_x(s)=x^{s+1}/(s(s+1))$. The signed finite-height formula shifts from real part 2 to $1/4$. Each enclosed zero contributes $-m_\rho K_x(\rho)$, with its actual analytic multiplicity. Under primitive GRH, its norm is at most $4x\sqrt{x}\,m_\rho/(1+(\Im\rho)^2)$. Heights avoiding the zeros exist in every positive unit interval. The right-edge logarithmic derivative has norm at most 2, independently of the character, and the truncated integral converges to the full Perron integral. [Finite contour and zero sum](DirichletZeroSum.lean)

**Remaining analytic gap.** The theorem `miller_of_dirichletGRH_of_contourBounds` takes both archive GRH and `PrimitiveContourBounds`. To remove the second hypothesis, establish the following estimates with one absolute constant for all primitive nonprincipal characters of conductor $q$, all $x\ge16$, and arbitrarily large common heights:

1. Bound the sum of $m_\rho/(1+(\Im\rho)^2)$ inside the rectangle by $B\log q$. A sufficient route would be a conductor-uniform unit-window zero count of order $\log(q(|t|+2))$, summed against the inverse-square weight.
2. Bound each of the normalized left, top and bottom integrals separately by $B x\sqrt{x}\log q$. One route would control $L'/L$ on the left edge via a local partial-fraction expansion, and on both horizontal edges at heights quantitatively separated from the zeros. Qualitative avoidance alone gives no uniform bound. The proved right-edge convergence takes care of the truncation.

These inputs give a primitive triangular-sum bound with coefficient $A=7B$. If every positive integer through $x$ were a witness failure at level $N$, its character value would be one, hence a unit value agreeing with that of the primitive character. The sum would equal the untwisted sum, at least $x^2/16$ for $x\ge16$. Choosing $x=(16(A+1)\log N)^2$ contradicts the upper bound because $q\mid N$. This supplies a positive witness at any $N>2$ and transfers exactly the same cutoff to Miller's strong test. A witness may be a nonunit, as permitted by the statement. The constant 2 in the separate Bach target and a bit-operation cost model remain outside this reduction.
